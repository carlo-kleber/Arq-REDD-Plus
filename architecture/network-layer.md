# Network Layer

The Network Layer is responsible for communication among participants, transaction submission and propagation, and mechanisms related to participant identification, network access, and information protection. These responsibilities arise from the coexistence, within REDD+ projects, of information requiring broad auditability and data that may be subject to confidentiality requirements, such as contractual or financial information, personal data, and sensitive information associated with local communities.

To address these requirements, the Network Layer comprises four logical functional components:

- **Communication Management (N1):** responsible for supporting communication among participants and architectural components, including operation submission, message propagation, and the exchange of information required by the Consensus and Application layers.
- **Identity and Authentication (N2):** responsible for establishing and verifying the identities or credentials associated with participants and communicating entities. Depending on the selected configuration, this responsibility may involve cryptographic identities, digital certificates, or external identity management services. It also encompasses the management of credential validity and revocation, where applicable.
- **Network Access Control (N3):** responsible for enforcing the policies governing access to network resources and participation in network-level activities. This responsibility must be distinguished from authorization to execute domain-specific operations, such as approving MRV reports or issuing carbon credits, which is governed by the Application Layer.
- **Information Protection (N4):** responsible for supporting the confidentiality and integrity of information during communication and distribution, according to the applicable visibility and access policies. This responsibility may involve encryption, selective information disclosure, restricted transaction propagation, or other compatible protection mechanisms.

These components represent logical responsibilities rather than mandatory software modules. Their realization may involve native Blockchain mechanisms, external services, or combinations of both. Their interactions with the Consensus and Application layers must preserve the identity, authorization, and information protection requirements associated with the operations being processed.

The functional components of the Network Layer contribute to the requirements identified in Table 4 of the accompanying manuscript. In particular, Communication Management (N1) supports the exchange of MRV information and integration with external systems (TC2 and IT1); Identity and Authentication (N2) supports stakeholder identification and the attribution of operations and evidence to their respective sources (GP1 and TC2); Network Access Control (N3) supports the enforcement of participation and information access policies (GP1 and GP2); and Information Protection (N4) supports the protection of sensitive information, including records associated with socio-environmental safeguards (GP2 and GP3). Together, these components also contribute to the preservation of architectural responsibilities across compatible technological configurations (IT3).

The model does not prescribe a single network configuration. Instead, it distinguishes four complementary technological decision points: network visibility (DN1), participation permissions (DN2), information protection (DN3), and identity and authentication (DN4). Network visibility concerns the extent to which ledger information is publicly accessible or restricted. Participation permissions concern the conditions under which entities may access network services, submit transactions, or participate in validation and consensus activities. Information protection concerns the mechanisms employed to enforce the applicable confidentiality and disclosure requirements. Identity and authentication concern the mechanisms employed to establish and verify participant identities or credentials, including credential management and revocation, where applicable.

These dimensions must not be treated as mutually exclusive network categories. For example, a publicly readable ledger may coexist with restricted participation in transaction validation, while a permissioned network may support different levels of information visibility among its authorized participants.

A hybrid configuration may be employed when certain credit-related operations require broader auditability, whereas other information requires restricted access. In such a configuration, publicly accessible records may coexist with protected transaction data or off-chain information accessible only to authorized participants. Specific technologies for private transactions, including mechanisms based on trusted execution environments or transaction managers, represent possible implementations of this configuration but are not mandatory components of Arq-REDD+.

The selection of network and information protection mechanisms must consider their compatibility with the consensus mechanism, transaction validation procedures, identity management infrastructure, and application-level authorization policies. In particular, confidentiality mechanisms must not prevent the verification of the information required to establish valid ledger state transitions.

The architectural invariants of the Network Layer concern the preservation of its communication, identification, authentication, access control, and information protection responsibilities. Alternative technologies may be considered during system design, provided that these responsibilities and the associated interface contracts are preserved. This flexibility does not imply that technological mechanisms can necessarily be replaced after deployment without migration procedures or modifications to other implementation components.
