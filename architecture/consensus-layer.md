# Consensus Layer

The Consensus Layer is responsible for establishing agreement among participants on the valid state of the distributed ledger. Its architectural responsibilities arise from the need to maintain consistent and auditable records of project-related operations, particularly those associated with carbon credit issuance, transfer, retirement, cancellation, and reversal accounting.

To address these requirements, the Consensus Layer comprises four logical functional components:

- **Transaction Validation (C1):** responsible for verifying the admissibility of transactions according to the rules of the selected Blockchain protocol and the applicable state transition conditions. This responsibility includes verifying protocol-level requirements and considering the results of domain-specific validation performed by the Application Layer. It does not encompass the independent assessment of the environmental validity of MRV information.
- **Distributed Agreement (C2):** responsible for coordinating the participating entities according to the selected consensus mechanism to establish agreement on transaction ordering or valid ledger state transitions, subject to the operating and fault assumptions of the protocol.
- **Ledger State Management (C3):** responsible for maintaining and updating the distributed ledger according to the accepted state transitions. This responsibility encompasses the preservation of consistent ledger records and the availability of the information required to verify their integrity, according to the applicable access and visibility policies.
- **Finality Management (C4):** responsible for determining and communicating the conditions under which a transaction or ledger state transition can be considered confirmed or finalized. These conditions depend on the properties of the selected consensus mechanism and may involve deterministic or probabilistic finality.

These components represent logical responsibilities rather than mandatory software modules. Depending on the selected Blockchain platform and consensus mechanism, transaction validation, distributed agreement, ledger state management, and finality determination may be performed by overlapping implementation modules or through different execution sequences.

The functional components of the Consensus Layer contribute to the requirements identified in Table 4 of the accompanying manuscript. Transaction Validation (C1) supports the enforcement of valid credit life-cycle transitions and the rejection of invalid or duplicate operations according to the rules specified by the Application Layer (TC3 and TC5). Distributed Agreement (C2) supports agreement on the valid state of the distributed ledger (CI1). Ledger State Management (C3) supports the consistent recording of credit life-cycle transitions, reversal-related accounting adjustments, and auditable information (TC3, TC4, and CI2). Finality Management (C4) supports the determination of the confirmation status of credit-related operations and the conditions under which ledger state transitions may be considered final (TC3 and CI1). Together, these components also contribute to the preservation of architectural responsibilities across compatible technological configurations (IT3).

The selection of a consensus mechanism constitutes a technological decision point in Arq-REDD+. Different consensus families and protocols may be considered, including mechanisms based on computational work, stake, and Byzantine fault-tolerant voting. These alternatives involve different assumptions concerning participant eligibility, trust, fault tolerance, resource consumption, communication overhead, scalability, and transaction confirmation.

The selection process must consider the safety and liveness properties of the candidate mechanisms under their respective operating assumptions. Safety concerns the preservation of agreement and the validity of accepted ledger states, whereas liveness concerns the ability of the system to continue processing valid operations under the conditions assumed by the protocol. These properties must be distinguished from performance characteristics, such as transaction throughput and confirmation latency.

Another relevant technological decision concerns the finality conditions adopted by the implementation. Depending on the selected mechanism, transaction finality may be deterministic or probabilistic. The Application Layer must account for these differences when determining whether a credit-related operation can be treated as completed. In particular, transaction submission or inclusion in a block does not necessarily imply that the corresponding operation has reached the required level of finality.

The selection of a consensus mechanism is not independent of the technological decisions associated with the Network and Application layers. Network participation policies must be compatible with the eligibility and trust assumptions of the consensus mechanism. Information protection mechanisms must preserve the availability of the information required for transaction validation. Application-level execution and validation procedures must also be compatible with the ordering, state transition, and finality properties provided by the selected mechanism.

Although the Consensus Layer contributes to the consistency and integrity of recorded information, distributed agreement does not independently guarantee the correctness of external data, the environmental validity of carbon credits, or the legitimacy of decisions made outside the Blockchain system. These aspects depend on the domain-specific validation procedures, external information sources, and governance mechanisms supported by the other architectural components.

The architectural invariants of the Consensus Layer concern the preservation of transaction validation, distributed agreement, ledger state management, and finality determination responsibilities. Different consensus mechanisms may be selected during system design, provided that their operating assumptions and properties satisfy the applicable requirements and compatibility constraints while preserving the associated interface contracts. This flexibility does not imply that consensus mechanisms provide equivalent performance, security, or fault tolerance, or that they can be replaced after deployment without affecting other implementation components.
